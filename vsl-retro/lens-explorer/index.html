Overall score

Blends three ratings, each from 0 to 100 against the 27 lenses here:

Oxygen & moisture: how close the lens sits to the chart’s top-right corner. The closest lens scores 100 and the farthest 0. The dotted curves join points equally close to the corner.
Infection & inflammation safety: 100 for daily disposables (lowest risk) and 50 for reusable lenses (medium risk).
Price: cost per day of wear compared across all lenses, from 100 for the cheapest to 0 for the most expensive. Daily disposables cost more per day than reusable lenses, so they rate lower here even when their $ rating is typical for daily lenses.

By default, eye health counts for 80% (oxygen & moisture 40%, safety 40%) and price for 20%. Use the sliders to set your own priorities.

Infection & inflammation risk

Contact lens infections are rare, but they can damage vision. The rating combines three risks, each compared between daily disposables and reusable lenses worn during the day:

Serious infection: about 2× higher with reusable lenses (1.1 vs. 0.5 moderate-to-severe infections per 10,000 wearers a year).
Acanthamoeba infection: about 4× higher. This rare infection is hard to treat and is linked to lens cases and water.
Inflammatory reactions (red, sore eyes with spots on the cornea): about 10× higher.

Daily disposables are lowest on all three, because there’s no storage case or solution to carry germs, so they rate Lowest. Reusable lenses rate Medium; research hasn’t shown a difference between 1-week, 2-week and monthly lenses. Sleeping in any lens raises the risk the most, roughly tenfold. Milder infections happen at similar rates with all lenses, so good habits still matter.

Oxygen (Dk/t)

The cornea, the clear front of your eye, gets most of its oxygen straight from the air. Dk/t measures how easily oxygen passes through a lens of a typical prescription (−3.00). A higher number lets more oxygen through.

The scorecard colours use research benchmarks: about 35 Dk/t keeps the cornea supplied while your eyes are open, and 87 to 125 is the level researchers set for when the eyes are closed, the toughest case.

Water content

The share of the lens that is water, by weight. Water helps keep a lens soft and flexible on the eye. Lenses at 50% or more are classed as “high water” lenses, which is where the scorecard turns green.

* Dailies Total1, Precision1 and Total30 have a lower-water core under a much wetter surface layer. The chart plots the core value.

Why most lenses trade one for the other

Older-style hydrogel lenses carry oxygen through their water, so even very watery ones let through far less oxygen than silicone hydrogels. Silicone hydrogels carry oxygen through silicone, which usually means less water.

Keep in mind

Comfort also depends on how a lens fits, its surface, how often you replace it, your tears and how you care for your lenses. Manufacturers don’t all measure the same way, so treat small differences as a tie. Your optometrist can help you weigh what matters for your eyes.
